I am learning Portuguese in Chengdu, which is a polite way of saying I am
learning it from books. No conversation partners, no evening classes: pages, a
dictionary, and the stubborn feeling of reading through a pane of glass. For a
long time I treated that slowness as a handicap. It has since become my method,
and then my subject: reading in a language you are learning changes what reading
is.

## The sentence that refuses to stop

In Saramago, the sentence does not know the full stop. It moves forward by
commas, swallows dialogue, declines to say who is speaking, and starts again. In
_Memorial do convento_ — _Baltasar and Blimunda_ in English — a single period
can cross a whole page: you walk into a sentence in Baltasar’s company and walk
out beside Blimunda without ever passing a door. The translations I kept at hand
used to "fix" this, with periods, dashes, order. I closed them the day I
understood that what they were repairing was exactly what I had come for.

> Se podes olhar, vê. Se podes ver, repara.

The epigraph of _Ensaio sobre a cegueira_ — _Blindness_ — runs eight words, and
those eight words defeat translation: _if you can look, see; if you can see,
notice_. English flattens _olhar_ and _ver_; Portuguese learned slowly lets each
keep its weight. The long sentence is not the mannerism of an old master: it is
a theory of time. The world does not stop at the edges of our sentences, so why
should the sentence stop?

## One man containing several

Pessoa poses the opposite problem. Saramago melts every voice into one; Pessoa
splits his own into four, then dozens. Alberto Caeiro, the pastoral _master_ who
wrote that things have no meaning — they have existence. Ricardo Reis, the
physician and classicist of odes. Álvaro de Campos, the engineer who smoked and
was bored with genius. Bernardo Soares, the assistant bookkeeper who kept the
_Livro do desassossego_, _The Book of Disquiet_. The word _heteronym_ is not a
critic’s refinement: each one has a style, a biography, sometimes a death. To
translate Pessoa is to be forced to choose a single voice for four.

> O meu país é a língua portuguesa.

_My country is the Portuguese language._ I read that line of Bernardo Soares in
a city where I speak Chinese at the market, English at work, and French to
myself — and I understand what it promises: a homeland that fits in a pocket.
But I also read it as a foreigner, and I know what it costs.

## Reading slowly

This is what Portuguese teaches me in Chengdu: slowness is not what remains to
be fixed; it is what remains to be read. Saramago forces me to follow the
sentence instead of guessing it; Pessoa forces me to ask _who_ is speaking
before asking _what_. In my own language I read too fast to hear those
questions. In theirs, I have no choice.
