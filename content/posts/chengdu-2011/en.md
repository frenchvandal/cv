---
title: Landing in Chengdu, 2011
date: 2025-08-20
summary: Two suitcases, three words of Chinese and the smell of chili oil at the aircraft door. Fourteen years ago I landed in Chengdu — and I never really left.
tags: [chine, récit]
---

The plane touched down at Shuangliu on an August night in 2011, and the heat
came into the cabin before the doors even opened. A thick, humid heat that
smelled of chili oil and Sichuan pepper. I thought it was drifting over from the
airport kitchens; it was drifting over from the whole city. I had two suitcases,
a work contract and three words of Chinese picked up on the flight, already
mispronounced.

Chengdu already had more than ten million people, but the city had not yet stood
up. One metro line, Line 1, opened the year before, which locals pointed out
with a slightly incredulous pride. Rivers of bicycles and silent electric
scooters flowed down the avenues, and at every crossroads a repair man squatted
among his lengths of inner tube. Everything was paid in cash, notes rolled up in
your pocket, and taxis honked before overtaking, never after.

The disorientation was not a shock, more a slow and gentle drowning. First the
language: Sichuanese sounded nothing like anything I had heard, a Mandarin put
through the south-western grinder where my three words did not survive. Then the
food. At my first hotpot my lips started to buzz as if wired to a battery. I
thought it was an allergic reaction; it was 麻, the numbing power of Sichuan
pepper, half the soul of this cuisine.

Everything exceeded me by half a tone. Children shouted hello at the sight of
me, strangers invited me to their weddings. The sky stayed grey for weeks —
there is a proverb, 蜀犬吠日: the dogs of Sichuan bark when the sun comes out,
so unfamiliar is the sight. At night the click of mahjong tiles rose from the
inner courtyards, and in the afternoon, in People’s Park, old men drank jasmine
tea in bamboo chairs while the ear cleaners made their tweezers chime. The 2008
earthquake was still in every conversation; people showed you the cracks the way
you show scars.

Fourteen years on, I only recognise the city by its mood. The metro has passed
five hundred kilometres, a second airport has grown out of the southern plain,
and the glass towers of Tianfu have eaten the fields where I used to ride on
Sundays. The bicycle repair men have vanished, replaced by shared bikes you
unlock with a phone — the same phone that pays for everything, from hotpot to
the fruit stall. I have not touched a banknote in years, and the hotpot chains
from here have opened branches in Paris, which still makes me smile.

And yet, under the glass and steel, Chengdu has not changed its soul. The
teahouses are full, the mahjong still clicks, and people still say 巴适 —
comfortable, just right — as they sink into their chairs. The estrangement I
boarded that plane looking for, I have lived in it so long it has become my
country.
