title: Eighty kilometres of patience

I signed up on a November evening, the day after a half marathon that had gone
reasonably well. Eighty kilometres, 2,800 metres of climbing, a midnight start.
On paper it was one more row in a spreadsheet; in truth I had no idea what a
whole night of running costs.

The preparation took twenty-two weeks, and I ran it like a work project: a
versioned training plan, weekly volumes, sessions labelled "key" or
"supporting". The business analyst in me loved it. Three long runs a month,
never more than ten percent added from one week to the next, and a race kit
rehearsed three times, down to the socks.

The gear itself was a short list:

- an 8-litre vest with two half-litre soft flasks;
- a 400-lumen headlamp, plus a spare battery;
- a 140-gram rain shell, mandatory under the race rules;
- poles, stowed at the start, out at the first climb;
- six gels, most of which I never touched.

The night was the real race. Between kilometre 25 and 55 there is nothing: no
spectators, no scenery, just the pool of lamplight and the sound of my own
steps. I crossed two sleeping villages, startled a fox, and walked — yes, walked
— every steep climb. That is where I learned the rule of ultras: you don't race
the others, you manage a budget.

The aid stations were my milestones. I kept a simple discipline there:

- fill the flasks first, eat second, leave before getting cold;
- something salty at every stop, something sweet only if the stomach agreed;
- nothing new on race day — the noodle soup at km 58 had been rehearsed in
  training.

What do 80 kilometres teach about patience? That effort is not measured at any
single moment but budgeted over a duration nothing lets you guess. My heart rate
lied on every climb; only a pace held for hours told the truth. I brought back
three rules, equally valid on Monday morning at the office:

- start slower than you think you can sustain;
- measure to make decisions, not to reassure yourself;
- deal with a problem at the first symptom, never at the next kilometre.

I finished at dawn, in a little under eleven hours. No transcendence, just a
deep tiredness and the quiet satisfaction of a spreadsheet whose numbers, for
once, were all true.
